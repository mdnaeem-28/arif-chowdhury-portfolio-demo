BOOKING UX

HOTEL BOOKING REDESIGN

Demo case study. Fewer steps from search to confirmation, found by watching ten travelers book on their phones.

Demo case studyFictional client: a hotel booking app

Problem

Travelers needed 6.4 minutes on average to book a room, and many gave up between the room page and the confirmation. The flow asked for the same details twice and showed the full price only at the very end.

My role and team

Arif, product designer. Owned research, journeys, flows and screens. Rafi Ahmed, engineering lead, checked every flow for build effort before it moved on.

Process

  1. Discover. Watched ten travelers book a room on their phones and noted every pause.
  2. Define. Mapped the booking journey and marked each step that asked for something twice.
  3. Design. Redrew the flow from search to receipt, edge cases included, and tested a clickable prototype.
  4. Deliver. Handed over specs and states, then reviewed the build with engineering in two rounds.

Key screens

  • 1

    Search results. Price and room type are visible before any tap.

  • 2

    Room page. The total with taxes sits next to the book button.

  • 3

    Confirmation. Dates, address and a change option on one screen.

Neutral placeholders for the demo. The photo above shows the three screens together.

Outcome

3.1 min

What I learned

Showing the total price early removed more doubt than any visual polish. Watching people book beats debating it in a meeting.

Demo case studyFictional client: a train ticket app

Problem

Buyers could pay by card, mobile wallet or a cash voucher, but the payment flow took seven steps and failed without saying why. When a code was rejected, most people left the app and did not come back.

My role and team

Arif, product designer. Owned the payment flow, error states and handover. Sara Khan, engineering lead, built the payment integration and flagged edge cases early.

Process

  1. Discover. Read support tickets and listed every place a payment could fail.
  2. Define. Set two goals: fewer steps, and a plain message for every failure.
  3. Design. Drew seat selection, payment methods and the ticket, then wrote the copy for each error.
  4. Deliver. Shipped a state list with the specs and checked each state in the build.

Key screens

  • 1

    Seat selection. Free, taken and chosen seats read clearly without a legend.

  • 2

    Payment methods. One list, with the fastest method first.

  • 3

    Ticket. A large QR code and the boarding details, usable offline.

Neutral placeholders for the demo. The photo above shows the three screens together.

What I learned

Errors are part of the flow and need design time from day one. Fewer steps only help when each remaining step says what happens next.
